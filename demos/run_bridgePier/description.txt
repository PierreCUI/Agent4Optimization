Bridge Pier under the Deck Bearing

This is the bridge pier problem of highway and railway bridges: a
massive concrete column stands on its foundation, carrying the bridge
deck through a bearing seat on its top and its own enormous weight,
while the deck also shoves the pier horizontally when vehicles brake
or the deck expands. The pier is a
long prism whose cross-section is a rectangle, so the problem is
treated as plane strain in that cross-section.

The problem is a topology optimization on a rectangle 2 m wide and 1 m
tall. The task is to find the best material layout inside the
rectangle: solid regions may be added or removed, and the design is
described by a level-set function, negative where the material is solid
and positive where it is void. The physics is steady, linear elasticity
with one single physical field, the displacement u, a vector with a
horizontal and a vertical component, measured in metres. The solid is a
linear elastic material with Young's modulus 200 N/m^2 and Poisson's
ratio 0.3; regions without material are treated as a weak elastic solid
100 times softer than the real material. The optimization starts from a
prescribed initial design: a checkerboard pattern of solid and void
cells covering the whole rectangle, roughly half solid.

The bottom edge is the foundation: it is held
completely fixed, its horizontal and vertical displacements both zero.
The top edge carries the deck: its central 0.4 m, the bearing seat, is
pressed downward by a uniform traction of 10 N/m^2 perpendicular to the
edge, and at the same time shoved sideways by a braking traction of
3 N/m^2 acting along the edge, parallel to it; the flanking parts of
the top edge on both sides of the seat are free. The left edge and the
right edge are free. Besides the deck loads, the solid material is
pulled downward by its own weight, a uniform body force of 10 N/m^3
acting throughout the volume of every solid region. The fixed bottom
edge and the bearing seat are
non-design boundaries: their positions are prescribed, they are frozen,
and the optimizer cannot add or remove material on them; the free
flanks of the top edge and the two lateral edges are design
boundaries, where material may be attached or removed freely. The
optimization goal is to minimize the elastic strain energy stored in
the pier under the deck loads and its own weight — its compliance —
while using as little
material as possible, a balance between stiffness and material cost.
