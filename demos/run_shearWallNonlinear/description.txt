Shear Wall of a Strain-Hardening Composite

This is the shear wall problem of seismic and wind design with
fibre-reinforced concrete: a stiff wall stands on the foundation and
receives the whole lateral force of the floors above, and the
composite material it is built from grows stiffer wherever it deforms
most, helping the wall carry the sideways shove down into the ground.
The wall is a long prism whose cross-section is a rectangle, so the
problem is treated as plane strain in that cross-section.

The problem is a topology optimization on a rectangle 2 m wide and 1 m
tall. The task is to find the best material layout inside the
rectangle: solid regions may be added or removed, and the design is
described by a level-set function, negative where the material is solid
and positive where it is void. The physics is steady, nonlinear
elasticity with one single physical field, the displacement u, a vector
with a horizontal and a vertical component, measured in metres. The
solid is a strain-hardening fibre-reinforced composite: its stiffness
grows as it deforms, being 200 N/m^2 where the material is undeformed
and doubling at a strain of one fifth — the modulus rises in
proportion to the square of the local strain magnitude; this
dependence makes the state problem nonlinear. Regions without material
are treated as a weak elastic solid 100 times softer than the solid at
the same deformation state. The optimization starts from a prescribed
initial design: a checkerboard pattern of solid and void cells covering
the whole rectangle, roughly half solid.

The bottom edge is the foundation: it is held
completely fixed, its horizontal and vertical displacements both zero.
The top edge receives the lateral force from the floor diaphragm above:
a uniform traction of 10 N/m^2 acting along the edge, parallel to it,
shoving the wall sideways. The left edge and the right edge are free.
The fixed bottom edge and the loaded top edge are non-design
boundaries: their positions are prescribed, they are frozen, and the
optimizer cannot add or remove material on them; the two lateral edges
are design boundaries, where material may be attached or removed
freely. The optimization goal is to minimize the elastic strain energy
stored in the wall under the lateral load — its compliance — while
using as little material as possible, a balance between stiffness and
material cost.
