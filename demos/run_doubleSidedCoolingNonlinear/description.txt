Double-Sided Cooling of a Power Module with Temperature-Dependent Heat Generation

This is the double-sided cooling architecture of power semiconductor
modules (IGBT or SiC): the die region, modeled as a volumetric heat
source, is sandwiched between two cold plates, so heat leaves the
module through both the top and the bottom face, while the lateral
faces are encapsulated and insulated. The die is a semiconductor
device whose leakage current — and therefore its self-heating — grows
with temperature, so the hottest regions generate the most heat.

The problem is a topology optimization on a square plate of side 1 m.
The task is to find the best material layout inside the plate: solid
regions may be added or removed, and the design is described by a
level-set function, negative where the material is solid and positive
where it is void. The physics is steady, nonlinear heat conduction with
one single physical field, the temperature T in kelvin. The solid
material has a thermal conductivity k of 1 W/(m K); regions without
material are treated as a weak heat conductor that conducts 10 times
less than the solid. The volumetric heat source is not fixed: it
strengthens with the local temperature, being 10 times the quantity 1
plus 0.1 times the local temperature in kelvin, in W/m^3 — the
leakage-current self-heating of the semiconductor; this temperature
feedback makes the state problem nonlinear. The optimization starts
from a prescribed initial design: a checkerboard pattern of solid and
void cells covering the whole plate, roughly half solid.

The temperature-dependent volumetric heat source described above acts
everywhere in the domain. The bottom edge and the top edge are held at
the fixed temperature 0: they are the two cold plates; the left edge
and the right edge are insulated, so that no heat crosses them. Both
cold edges are non-design boundaries: they are frozen, and the
optimizer cannot add or remove material on them; the two insulated
lateral edges are design boundaries, where material may be attached or
removed freely. The optimization goal is to minimize the total thermal
energy dissipated by heat conduction in the plate while using as little
material as possible — a balance between conduction performance and
material cost.
