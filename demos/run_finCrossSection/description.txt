Convection-Cooled Fin Heated by a Boundary Flux

This is the textbook cooling-fin design problem of electronic devices
such as LED lamps and power supplies: the device die's heat enters the
fin as a prescribed heat flux through the fin root, and the fin rejects
it by natural or forced convection from its surface. There is no
internal heat generation — the load is purely the boundary flux at the
root.

The problem is a topology optimization on a square plate of side 1 m.
The task is to find the best material layout inside the plate: solid
regions may be added or removed, and the design is described by a
level-set function, negative where the material is solid and positive
where it is void. The physics is steady, linear heat conduction with
one single physical field, the temperature T in kelvin. The solid
material has a thermal conductivity k of 1 W/(m K); regions without
material are treated as a weak heat conductor that conducts 10 times
less than the solid. The optimization starts from a prescribed initial
design: a checkerboard pattern of solid and void cells covering the
whole plate, roughly half solid.

There is no internal heat generation: the bottom edge is the fin root,
fed by the device die with a prescribed inward heat flux of 10 W/m^2,
and the remaining three edges — left, top and right — lose heat by
convection to the surrounding air at temperature 0, with a heat
transfer coefficient h of 1 W/(m^2 K); no part of the boundary is held
at a fixed temperature. The flux-loaded root is a non-design boundary:
its position is prescribed, it is frozen, and the optimizer cannot add
or remove material on it; the three convecting faces are design
boundaries, where material may be attached or removed freely. The
optimization goal is to minimize the total thermal energy dissipated
by heat conduction in the plate while using as little material as
possible — a balance between conduction performance and material cost.
