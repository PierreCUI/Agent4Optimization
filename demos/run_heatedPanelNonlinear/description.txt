Heat Spreading in a Flux-Heated Panel with Temperature-Dependent Conductivity

This is the flux-heated panel problem of solar photovoltaics and power
module baseplates: the panel's top surface absorbs a uniform heat flux
(sunlight or die contact), and the panel must conduct all that heat to
the cooled bottom edge, where the heat sink is mounted. There is no
internal heat generation — the load enters purely through the loaded
boundary. The panel is a polymer-based composite whose thermal
conductivity improves as it warms — the hotter a region is, the
better it conducts.

The problem is a topology optimization on a square plate of side 1 m.
The task is to find the best material layout inside the plate: solid
regions may be added or removed, and the design is described by a
level-set function, negative where the material is solid and positive
where it is void. The physics is steady, nonlinear heat conduction with
one single physical field, the temperature T in kelvin. The solid
material's thermal conductivity is not constant: it grows as the panel
heats, being 1 plus 0.2 times the local temperature in kelvin, in
W/(m K) — the behaviour of polymer-based composites; this temperature
dependence makes the state problem nonlinear. Regions
without material are treated as a weak heat conductor that conducts 10
times less than the solid at the same temperature. The optimization
starts from a prescribed initial design: a checkerboard pattern of
solid and void cells covering the whole plate, roughly half solid.

There is no internal heat generation: the top edge receives a
prescribed inward heat flux of 10 W/m^2 over its whole length, so heat
enters the panel through it; the bottom edge is held at the fixed
temperature 0, the cold mount; the left edge and the right edge are
insulated, so that no heat crosses them. The flux-loaded top edge and
the cold bottom edge are non-design boundaries: their positions are
prescribed, they are frozen, and the optimizer cannot add or remove
material on them; the two insulated lateral edges are design
boundaries, where material may be attached or removed freely. The
optimization goal is to minimize the total thermal energy dissipated
by heat conduction in the plate while using as little material as
possible — a balance between conduction performance and material cost.
