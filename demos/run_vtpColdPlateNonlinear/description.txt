Cold Plate for a Uniformly Heated Package with Temperature-Dependent Conductivity

This is the classic volume-to-point heat conduction problem of
electronics cooling: heat is generated uniformly inside a power
electronics package, and all of it must be carried to one small cold
spot — the contact area of the attached heat sink. This benchmark
configuration was introduced by Bejan and is widely used in heat
conduction topology optimization. Here the plate material is a
semiconductor whose ability to conduct heat fades as it heats up, so
the hottest regions conduct worse than the cold ones.

The problem is a topology optimization on a square plate of side 1 m.
The task is to find the best material layout inside the plate: solid
regions may be added or removed, and the design is described by a
level-set function, negative where the material is solid and positive
where it is void. The physics is steady, nonlinear heat conduction with
one single physical field, the temperature T in kelvin. The solid
material's thermal conductivity is not constant: it falls as the
material heats, being 1 divided by 1 plus 0.1 times the local
temperature in kelvin, in W/(m K) — the behaviour of silicon and other
semiconductors; this temperature dependence makes the state problem
nonlinear. Regions without material are treated as a weak heat
conductor that conducts 10 times less than the solid at the same
temperature. The optimization starts from a prescribed initial design:
a checkerboard pattern of solid and void cells covering the whole
plate, roughly half solid.

Heat is generated uniformly inside the plate, with a volumetric heat
source of 10 W/m^3 everywhere in the domain. The central portion of
the left edge, between the heights 0.4 m and 0.6 m, is held at the
fixed temperature 0: it is the cold patch where the heat sink is
attached; every remaining part of the boundary is insulated, so that
no heat crosses it. The cold patch is a non-design boundary: it is
frozen, and the optimizer cannot add or remove material on it; all the
other edges are design boundaries, where material may be attached or
removed freely. The optimization goal is to minimize the total thermal
energy dissipated by heat conduction in the plate while using as
little material as possible — a balance between conduction performance
and material cost.
