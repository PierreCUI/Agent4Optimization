Bridge Deck under Traffic, Temperature Gradient and Self-Weight

This is the temperature-gradient problem of bridge decks, the
classical design load of EN 1991-1-5: the top surface of a concrete
deck slab absorbs the sun and heats up far above the shaded underside,
so a strong vertical temperature gradient stands across the slab while
the traffic load and the slab's own weight bend it between the edge
girders. The deck is a long prism whose cross-section is a rectangle,
so the problem is treated as plane strain in that cross-section.

The problem is a topology optimization on a rectangle 4 m wide and 1 m
tall. The task is to find the best material layout inside the
rectangle: solid regions may be added or removed, and the design is
described by a level-set function, negative where the material is solid
and positive where it is void. The physics is steady: linear heat
conduction for the temperature T, measured in kelvin, and linear
elasticity for the displacement u, a vector with a horizontal and a
vertical component, measured in metres; the two are coupled one-way —
the temperature drives a thermal expansion strain of 3.6e-5 per kelvin
times the temperature, subtracted from the elastic strain, so a heated
solid region expands and pushes against its supports. The solid
material has Young's modulus 3.2e7 N/m^2, Poisson's ratio 0.23, density
2500 kg/m^3, thermal conductivity 1.25 W/(m K) and thermal expansion
coefficient 3.6e-5 per kelvin; regions without material are treated as
a weak phantom that is 1000 times softer, 1000 times less conductive
and 1000 times less expansive than the solid. The optimization starts
from a prescribed initial design: a checkerboard pattern of solid and
void cells covering the rectangle, roughly half solid, with a thin
solid strip along the top edge.

The top edge is the sun-lit pavement: it receives a prescribed inward
heat flux of 800 W/m^2, the absorbed solar radiation, and it carries a
uniform traction of 5000 N/m^2 pressing downward, the traffic load. The
bottom edge is the shaded soffit: it is held at the fixed temperature
20, the cool shaded air beneath, and it is free of any mechanical load.
The left edge and the right edge are the girder seats: each is held in
place by strong penalty springs that pull its horizontal and vertical
displacements back to zero, for the slab sits on the edge girders, and
each is insulated, so that no heat crosses it. Besides the traffic
load, the solid material is pulled downward by its own weight, a
uniform body force of 24500 N/m^3 acting throughout the volume of every
solid region; there is no body heat. The sun-lit pavement and the two
girder seats are non-design boundaries: their positions are prescribed,
they are frozen, and the optimizer cannot add or remove material on
them; the shaded soffit is a design boundary, where material may be
attached or removed freely. The optimization goal is to minimize the
elastic strain energy stored in the deck under the traffic load, the
vertical temperature gradient and its own weight — its compliance —
while using as little material as possible, a balance between stiffness
and material cost, and a strong penalty keeps the girder seats from
sliding horizontally.
