Cold Plate with Two Cooling Patches on Opposite Edges

This is the two-sink cooling problem of double-sided board assembly:
components spread over a PCB generate heat uniformly, and two cold
patches on opposite edges — two heat sink mounting pads or thermal via
fields — are the only escape paths. The design must choose which sink
each region feeds. This is the classical two-sink heat-spreading
configuration of electronics packaging.

The problem is a topology optimization on a square plate of side 1 m.
The task is to find the best material layout inside the plate: solid
regions may be added or removed, and the design is described by a
level-set function, negative where the material is solid and positive
where it is void. The physics is steady, linear heat conduction with
one single physical field, the temperature T in kelvin. The solid
material has a thermal conductivity k of 1 W/(m K); regions without
material are treated as a weak heat conductor that conducts 10 times
less than the solid. The optimization starts from a prescribed initial
design: a checkerboard pattern of solid and void cells covering the
whole plate, roughly half solid.

Heat is generated uniformly inside the plate, with a volumetric heat
source of 10 W/m^3 everywhere in the domain. There are two cold
patches, both held at the fixed temperature 0: one on the central
portion of the left edge, between the heights 0.4 m and 0.6 m, and one
on the central portion of the right edge, also between the heights
0.4 m and 0.6 m; every remaining part of the boundary is insulated, so
that no heat crosses it. Both cold patches are non-design boundaries:
they are frozen, and the optimizer cannot add or remove material on
them; all the insulated edges are design boundaries, where material
may be attached or removed freely. The optimization goal is to
minimize the total thermal energy dissipated by heat conduction in the
plate while using as little material as possible — a balance between
conduction performance and material cost.
