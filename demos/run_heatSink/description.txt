Heat Sink under Chip Heating and Mounting Pressure

This is the heat sink problem of LED lighting and CPU packaging: a
chip is bonded to the centre of the base plate and pours its waste
heat into the metal, which conducts the heat away and sheds it to the
air by convection from the finned face, while the assembled module
presses down on the sink with the mounting force of its clamp.

The problem is a topology optimization on a rectangle 4 m wide and 1 m
tall. The task is to find the best material layout inside the
rectangle: solid regions may be added or removed, and the design is
described by a level-set function, negative where the material is solid
and positive where it is void. The physics is steady: linear heat
conduction for the temperature T, measured in kelvin, and linear
elasticity for the displacement u, a vector with a horizontal and a
vertical component, measured in metres; the two are coupled one-way —
the temperature drives a thermal expansion strain of 3.6e-5 per kelvin
times the temperature, subtracted from the elastic strain, so a heated
solid region expands and pushes against its supports. The solid
material has Young's modulus 3.2e7 N/m^2, Poisson's ratio 0.23, density
2500 kg/m^3, thermal conductivity 1.25 W/(m K) and thermal expansion
coefficient 3.6e-5 per kelvin; regions without material are treated as
a weak phantom that is 1000 times softer, 1000 times less conductive
and 1000 times less expansive than the solid. The optimization starts
from a prescribed initial design: a checkerboard pattern of solid and
void cells covering the rectangle, roughly half solid, with a thin
solid strip along the top edge.

The central portion of the bottom edge, between the horizontal
positions 1.5 m and 2.5 m, is the chip contact face: it is held at the
fixed temperature 120, the hot chip, and it is held in place by strong
penalty springs that pull its horizontal and vertical displacements
back to zero, for the chip itself is rigid. The remaining parts of the
bottom edge, left and right of the chip, are insulated, so that no heat
crosses them, and they are free of any mechanical load. The top edge
loses heat by convection to the surrounding air at temperature 20, with
a heat transfer coefficient h of 100 W/(m^2 K), the fins shedding heat
to the air; the same top edge carries a uniform traction of 5000 N/m^2
pressing downward, the mounting force of the assembled module. The left
edge and the right edge are insulated and free. There is no body heat
and no body force. The chip contact face is a non-design boundary: its
position is prescribed, it is frozen, and the optimizer cannot add or
remove material on it; every other edge — the insulated parts of the
bottom edge, the convecting top edge and the two lateral edges — is a
design boundary, where material may be attached or removed freely. The
optimization goal is to minimize the elastic strain energy stored in
the sink under the mounting pressure and the thermal expansion of the
heated material — its compliance — while using as little material as
possible, a balance between stiffness and material cost, and a strong
penalty keeps the chip contact face from sliding horizontally.
