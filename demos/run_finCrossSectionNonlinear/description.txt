Convection- and Radiation-Cooled Fin Heated by a Boundary Flux

This is the textbook cooling-fin design problem of electronic devices
such as LED lamps and power supplies: the device die's heat enters the
fin as a prescribed heat flux through the fin root, and the fin rejects
it to the surrounding air through its exposed surfaces, partly by
natural or forced convection and partly by thermal radiation from the
fin surface. There is no internal heat generation — the load is purely
the boundary flux at the root.

The problem is a topology optimization on a square plate of side 1 m.
The task is to find the best material layout inside the plate: solid
regions may be added or removed, and the design is described by a
level-set function, negative where the material is solid and positive
where it is void. The physics is steady, nonlinear heat conduction with
one single physical field, the temperature T in kelvin. The solid
material has a thermal conductivity k of 1 W/(m K); regions without
material are treated as a weak heat conductor that conducts 10 times
less than the solid. The optimization starts from a prescribed initial
design: a checkerboard pattern of solid and void cells covering the
whole plate, roughly half solid.

There is no internal heat generation: the bottom edge is the fin root,
fed by the device die with a prescribed inward heat flux of 10 W/m^2.
The remaining three edges — left, top and right — lose heat to the
surrounding air at temperature 298 kelvin by two mechanisms acting
together: convection with a heat transfer coefficient of 25 W/(m^2 K),
and surface radiation following the Stefan-Boltzmann law, with a
surface emissivity of 0.8 and the Stefan-Boltzmann constant of 5.67e-8
W/(m^2 K^4) — the radiative loss grows with the fourth power of the
absolute surface temperature, which makes the state problem nonlinear;
at the working temperature rise of about 0.4 kelvin above the air, the
radiative share is roughly one fifth of the convective share, the
realistic proportion for a natural-convection heat sink. No part of
the boundary is held at a fixed temperature. The flux-loaded root is a
non-design boundary: its position is prescribed, it is frozen, and the
optimizer cannot add or remove material on it; the three convecting
faces are design boundaries, where material may be attached or removed
freely. The optimization goal is to minimize the total thermal energy
dissipated by heat conduction in the plate while using as little
material as possible — a balance between conduction performance and
material cost.
