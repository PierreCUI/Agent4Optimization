Pouch Battery Cell between Cooling Plates

This is the cooling problem of electric-vehicle battery modules: a
pouch cell generates heat uniformly as it charges and discharges, its
two broad faces are pressed against liquid-cooled cold plates, and the
whole module stack is held together by a compression preload acting on
those same faces. The cell is a long prism whose cross-section is a
rectangle, so the problem is treated as plane strain in that
cross-section.

The problem is a topology optimization on a rectangle 4 m wide and 1 m
tall. The task is to find the best material layout inside the
rectangle: solid regions may be added or removed, and the design is
described by a level-set function, negative where the material is solid
and positive where it is void. The physics is steady: linear heat
conduction for the temperature T, measured in kelvin, and linear
elasticity for the displacement u, a vector with a horizontal and a
vertical component, measured in metres; the two are coupled one-way —
the temperature drives a thermal expansion strain of 3.6e-5 per kelvin
times the temperature, subtracted from the elastic strain, so a heated
solid region expands and pushes against its supports. The solid
material has Young's modulus 3.2e7 N/m^2, Poisson's ratio 0.23, density
2500 kg/m^3, thermal conductivity 1.25 W/(m K) and thermal expansion
coefficient 3.6e-5 per kelvin; regions without material are treated as
a weak phantom that is 1000 times softer, 1000 times less conductive
and 1000 times less expansive than the solid. The optimization starts
from a prescribed initial design: a checkerboard pattern of solid and
void cells covering the rectangle, roughly half solid, with a thin
solid strip along the top edge.

Heat is generated uniformly inside every solid region, with a
volumetric heat source of 100 W/m^3 everywhere in the material, the
electrochemical heat of the cell. The left edge and the right edge are
the cooling faces: each loses heat by convection to the cold plates at
temperature 20, with a heat transfer coefficient h of 500 W/(m^2 K),
and each is at the same time pressed inward by a uniform traction of
5000 N/m^2, the compression preload of the module stack. The top edge
is insulated and free. The bottom edge is insulated, so that no heat
crosses it, and it sits on the support shelf: it is held in place by
strong penalty springs that pull its horizontal and vertical
displacements back to zero. There is no body force. The two cooled side
faces are non-design boundaries: their positions are prescribed, they
are frozen, and the optimizer cannot add or remove material on them;
the top edge and the bottom edge are design boundaries, where material
may be attached or removed freely. The optimization goal is to minimize
the elastic strain energy stored in the cell under the compression
preload and the thermal expansion of the heated cell — its compliance,
measured by the outward bulging of the two compressed faces — while
using as little material as possible, a balance between stiffness and
material cost, and a strong penalty keeps the support edge from sliding
horizontally.
